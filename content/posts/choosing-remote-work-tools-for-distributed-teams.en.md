Almost everything a distributed team needs already exists, costs very little, and does not need building. Chat, file storage, work tracking and access control can all be bought this afternoon on a monthly subscription. If an off-the-shelf tool solves your problem, we will tell you so and point you at it.

That leaves you three decisions: what your team actually repeats every week, the smallest stack that covers it, and whose name the accounts are in. For the wider order of operations, from fixing the process to picking the technology, see the [digital transformation roadmap for SMEs](/en/blog/digital-strategy/guides/sme-digital-transformation-roadmap/).

## Start from the work, not the tool category

Take a sheet of paper and write down five to seven things your team repeats every week. Not broad functions like "internal communication", but events you can point at. An admin takes an order and hands it to the warehouse. Someone asks for sign-off on a price. A new hire looks for last month's contract and has no idea which folder to open.

That list is what you carry into the decision. Every candidate tool gets judged on one question: if the handoff from admin to warehouse moved in here, how many steps does it take, and who is responsible at each one?

Tools bought off a feature comparison table usually end up unused. The features are all there, but nobody mapped a real handoff into them. Three weeks later the team is back in a WhatsApp group and the subscription keeps billing.

## The four jobs a remote stack has to cover

For almost any small team there are four jobs that need an obvious home.

- Talking, which covers daily messages and meetings. One place for quick messages, one for video calls.
- Storing files, with a single agreed official location. Important documents should not exist only on somebody's laptop.
- Tracking who is doing what, so the status of a job can be seen without asking.
- Controlling access, meaning who can open what, and how that access gets revoked when somebody leaves.

One tool per job is enough, and plenty of tools cover two at once. Trouble comes from overlap. If files can live in three places, staff will put them in three places, and nobody will know which version is current. The subscription cost rises, but the expensive part is time spent searching.

Before you add a fifth tool, check whether one of the four already covers the job.

## What a tool costs after the subscription price

The number on the pricing page is rarely the number you pay. Three things are worth working out before you sign up.

Per-seat pricing grows with headcount. A subscription that feels trivial for five people reads differently at twenty. Price your stack for the team size you expect in a year, not the one you have this month.

Data gets trapped. Ask before subscribing: if I move in a year, what format does my data come out in? If the answer is a PDF report, moving means retyping everything by hand.

Admin work always appears. Someone has to add users, set permissions and keep folders tidy. That work usually lands on whoever looks least busy, without ever being discussed. We go further into cost patterns that stay off the invoice in [the hidden cost of poor software choices](/en/blog/digital-strategy/hidden-cost-of-poor-software-choices-for-growth/).

Knowing the cost before committing is the decision owners most fear getting wrong. That fear applies to a pile of subscriptions exactly as it applies to a build.

## Keep the accounts in the company's name

Domains, the billing email, workspace admin and backups should all be registered to the business, not to a staff member's personal address. This is the cheapest insurance available, and it costs nothing at all if you do it on day one.

The practical shape is simple. Create one company address used only for service sign-ups, ideally on your own domain. Put the card or billing account in the company's name. You hold the admin account, or someone you genuinely trust with the keys does, and staff get ordinary accounts underneath.

What happens without that is predictable. The IT person who registered everything leaves, and your domain leaves with their personal inbox. Recovery takes time and sometimes does not work at all.

The same rule applies to software you commission. Code, servers, domains and every account sit in the client's name from day one rather than being handed over at the end, so nothing depends on us continuing to exist. The order for setting up access and backups is in [cloud adoption for growing businesses](/en/blog/digital-strategy/cloud-adoption-scalability-for-growing-businesses/), which is written for teams moving off files on the office computer.

## Tools do not fix a process that lives in one person's head

Plenty of operational processes run because one person has memorised the order. They know which orders need checking twice, which supplier to call first, and which document has to be attached or the invoice gets rejected. None of it was ever written down.

Moving that into a new tool does not resolve it. The same person stays the only one who understands it, now with a monthly bill and an interface everyone has to learn, so the fragility has just changed address.

Write the steps down first. One page per process is enough: who starts it, what gets checked at each stage, and who decides when something does not match. Writing it usually exposes two or three steps that were never necessary. Choose the tool after that, and the choice is far easier because you know what you are looking for.

## The signs a tool has stopped fitting

Off-the-shelf tools do not weaken gradually. They work fine until the business grows past their limits, and after that the signs are clear enough to count.

- A spreadsheet is being used to bridge two tools because they cannot talk to each other.
- The same data gets entered twice, say an order logged in the sales tool and retyped into the warehouse record.
- Workarounds exist that only your team knows about, like a notes field holding status because there is nowhere else to put it.
- The per-seat bill is large, and the annual figure is starting to look comparable to building something that fits.

All four can be seen and measured, so the decision does not rest on a feeling that the tool is "not quite right". If none of them is happening, your tool still fits and the money is better spent elsewhere.

## When a build is the right call, and when it is not

Custom software is expensive, which is why it only deserves to win in one situation: when the process is specific to how your business makes money. How you calculate partner commission, set delivery routes, or price per customer is usually not sitting in anyone's product.

Chat, file storage, invoicing and task tracking are not in that category, so buy them. Building your own means paying a lot to match something that already exists and is already maintained by somebody else.

If what you are facing really is a specific core process, start with a written plan and a fixed price for the first stage. Scope and price for each stage are agreed before that stage begins, so you can stop after any stage without an argument.
