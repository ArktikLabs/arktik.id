Before you buy or build a system, work out what happens on the day something breaks. The person who holds the process resigns, the vendor raises its price, or orders arrive faster than anyone can record them. A feature list is no help on that day.

Control is what helps. When the accounts, the data and the code are registered to your business, a disruption can be dealt with without waiting for anyone's permission. The order to digitise in is covered in the [roadmap for digitising a small or mid-sized business](/en/blog/digital-strategy/guides/sme-digital-transformation-roadmap/).

## What actually breaks when a business grows

Fragility rarely arrives as a system going dark. It usually looks smaller than that, and it happens far more often.

There is the sales spreadsheet only one person is willing to edit, because the formulas reference each other and nobody else knows which column can safely be moved. When that person takes a week off, the weekly numbers take the week off too. Somewhere else in the business there is a tool whose login still belongs to a staff member who left two years ago, with their personal email address sitting in the password recovery field.

The third pattern lives in a manual step that was never written down anywhere. Orders come in through a WhatsApp group, and one administrator copies them into the warehouse system every morning. The routine has worked for years, and nobody has ever documented it, because the person has always been there.

All three have the same shape: the technology is running normally while the business depends on a single person or a single account.

## Resilience is a question of control, not of features

Two companies can run identical software with very different risk. One of them holds the code, the servers, the domain and every account in its own name. The other holds a username and a password that a vendor issued.

In the first setup, a vendor that closes means you go looking for someone new to maintain a system that already exists, and your data and access stay where they are. In the second, a price rise or a server outage is someone else's decision and you receive the result.

That is why we put the code, servers, domain and every account in the client's name from day one rather than handing them over at the end of a project. No part of the business depends on us continuing to exist. What changes as load and headcount grow is covered in the piece on [cloud adoption and scalability](/en/blog/digital-strategy/cloud-adoption-scalability-for-growing-businesses/).

You can check one thing this week without help from anyone: open the invoices for your hosting, your domain and your email service, and read the name and address recorded on them.

## Adopt in stages you can stop

A large one-shot technology commitment stacks every risk at a single point. You sign a number for a whole system, pay some of it upfront, then wait months to find out whether the shape fits how your team works.

Cutting the commitment into stages is the safer route. Scope and price are agreed in writing before each stage begins, so the largest loss available to you is one stage, and if the direction turns out to be wrong you can stop after any stage without an argument.

Our first stage is called Discovery, it runs one to two weeks, and it produces a written plan with a fixed price. That document stays yours whether you continue or not. A proposal is only useful if you buy; a written plan is still useful if you walk away, including for getting quotes from someone else.

## Write down the process before you automate it

Software built on a process that exists only in one person's head inherits the same fragility. You have swapped one point of dependency for another that costs more to change.

Before anyone writes code, write the steps out one at a time: who does the work, how long it takes, and where the data comes from. Then write the part that usually gets skipped, which is the exceptions. Which customers are allowed to pay late, who can approve a discount above a certain figure, and what happens when stock is one unit short.

Record the reason behind each rule as well. The discount ceiling you use today might exist because the margin on one product category is thin, rather than because the number looked tidy. Without the reason, the next person changes it, because the rule looks arbitrary.

The same holds on the technical side. We keep the reasoning behind each decision alongside the code, so whoever inherits the system inherits the thinking and not just the files.

## Choose boring technology on purpose

Widely used technology can be maintained by someone you have not hired yet. The next developer has worked with it before, the problem you hit has already been written up by somebody else, and they can add one field in their first week without special training.

An unusual choice narrows the list of people who can keep the system alive. Sometimes it narrows to one name, and that name knows it. The cost never appears on the first invoice, because it only shows up on the second or third change request, once the original builder has moved to another job. Costs with that delay are broken down in the piece on [the hidden cost of poor software choices](/en/blog/digital-strategy/hidden-cost-of-poor-software-choices-for-growth/).

You can put the test without technical terms. If the developer who built this is unreachable next month, how many people in Indonesia could take it over? Conventional technology and recorded reasoning are what let a system outlive the people who built it, and that one question tests both at once.

## Sometimes the resilient answer is not to build

When your process already fits a tool on the market, buying that tool is the lower-risk decision, and we will say so. Custom software is expensive, and a price like that is only worth paying when there is no other route.

The direction of the adjustment separates the two. If you can adapt how you work slightly to fit an existing product, use the product. Custom work earns its cost when generic software forces the business to change shape, for instance when you would have to drop the per-customer pricing that is the reason your customers stay.

What needs protecting stays the same even for off-the-shelf tools. The account is in the company's name rather than a staff member's, and the data can be exported at any time. Try the export yourself once, instead of reading that the feature exists.

## A short test before you commit to anything

Five questions carry into any meeting, with a custom vendor or with a software salesperson.

Whose name is this account in, and who holds the recovery email? If the vendor closes six months from now, what stops and what keeps running? Besides the person working on it today, who else could maintain this? What is the smallest stage we can agree on now, and what do I keep if I stop after it? And last, where is the process written down, and can I open it myself without asking anyone for help?

If one answer sounds vague, settle that part before money moves. A longer set of questions for the briefing stage is in the piece on [what to ask before briefing a software vendor](/en/blog/digital-strategy/questions-to-ask-before-briefing-a-software-vendor/).
