You are probably reading this with a quote in front of you that has a design line on it, and no reliable way to tell whether that line buys anything you will be able to point to in six months. The return shows up in your operations rather than in how the screens look, and you can check for it before you commit.

Ask for the one thing that is hard to fake: working screens you can open in the first week or two, and every week after that. A design stage that ends with a deck gives you nothing to react to until the money is already spent.

## Where poor design shows up on your P&L

The cost of bad design is already on your books. It just arrives under other names: overtime, a support line, a discount to keep an annoyed customer, a hire you made because the current process needs a human to hold it together.

Look for it in places like these:

- Someone copies order details from the website into the stock spreadsheet every morning, and the two disagree by lunchtime.
- Customers call to ask whether their booking went through, because the confirmation screen does not say clearly enough.
- Two people in the office understand the request form, and one of them is on leave.
- Carts reach the payment step and stop there, and nobody can say which field caused it.

None of these get filed as design problems. They get filed as staffing, or support volume, or "we need a better system". The money leaves either way.

## Design is decisions about the work, not decoration

For custom software, the design stage is where you decide what the software does, who touches it, and in what order. Does the warehouse see an order before payment clears? Who is allowed to approve a discount, and does anyone get told when they do? What happens when a customer changes a delivery address after the driver has left?

Those are business decisions rather than visual ones, and they get made during design whether or not anyone writes them down. They are cheap to change while they are still a screen, and expensive once they are in code and in the habits of your staff.

Screens are how those decisions become visible to someone who cannot read code. A drawing of the approval step is something your operations lead can look at and say "that is not how we do it, the supervisor signs first", which is worth more than any amount of polish.

## Working screens beat a PDF

We deliver product design as working screens, not a document you approve and file away. A document describes a flow, while a clickable screen puts you in it. You press the button, you see what comes next, you notice that the form asks for a tax number you never collect at that point in the process.

That is where the expensive misunderstandings get caught. A written specification can be read and agreed by two people who picture completely different things, and neither finds out until the build is done. Screens close that gap early.

You can also see the design and the build as they happen, from the first week. There is a private preview link and a working demo each week, so the state of the work is something you check rather than something you are told about.

## Three questions that tell you if UX work is earning its price

Can you open it this week? Something you can click, rather than a status update or a summary of progress. If the answer is always "next week" or "once it is ready to show", you are buying on faith.

Does it match how the work is actually done? Show the screens to the person who does the job every day, not only to the person paying for the project. Operations leads spot the missing step quickly, and they will tell you which part of the flow exists because a customer once complained.

Does the reasoning behind each screen exist in writing? We record decisions and the thinking behind them alongside the code, so whoever maintains the software later inherits the thinking and not just the files. Without that, the next developer has to guess why the approval step has two stages, and guessing usually ends with the second stage being removed.

## When you should not pay for custom design

If an existing tool already fits your process, we will say so rather than design a replacement for it. Plenty of work that gets quoted as custom software is a well-understood process that a subscription tool handles properly: invoicing, scheduling, a basic shop, a help desk. Paying for a bespoke version of something you can rent buys you a maintenance obligation and very little else.

Custom design earns its price when the process is specific to your business and no existing tool matches it, or when the tool you use has become the reason your staff do manual work. Until you are sure which of those you have, the cheapest useful thing you can buy is a clear look at the process, which is what Discovery is for.

## What good design does after launch

The first thing you notice is quieter. Fewer people ring to ask what happens next, because the screen already told them. Fewer staff need a colleague standing behind them during their first week, because the order of the work on screen matches the order of the work in real life.

The second thing is slower to appear and worth more. Software built on conventional patterns, with the reasoning written down, can be handed to another developer without a month of archaeology first. You keep the option of changing who works on it, which is the practical form of not being locked in. For the argument about what that does to revenue and retention rather than to cost, we covered the growth side separately in [how UI/UX design supports business growth](/en/blog/design-ux/ui-ux-design-business-growth/).

## How to commission it without taking anyone's word for it

Stage the commitment by agreeing the scope and the price of the design stage before it starts, so you know what you are spending and what you get for it, and so the number cannot move halfway through. You can stop after the design stage if what you see does not fit the work, and you keep the plan either way.

Own everything from the first day. The design files, the repository, the domain, the hosting accounts, all in your name at the start rather than handed over at the end. Ownership promised at the end is a promise, while ownership at the start is something you can verify by logging in.

Then judge the stage on what you can see. If the screens match how your business runs and the reasoning is written down, continue. If they do not, you still have a plan, a price, and a clear reason to walk.
